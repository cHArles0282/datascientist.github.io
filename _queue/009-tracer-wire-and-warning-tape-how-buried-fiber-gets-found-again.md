A technician walks slowly along a grass verge holding a wand close to the ground, stopping every few steps to shake a can of orange paint and draw a line. Behind him, small orange flags trace a path across driveways and around a tree. He is mapping something he cannot see, and he can only do it because somebody years ago buried a piece of wire next to it.

Fiber optic cable is glass and plastic. An all-dielectric cable has no metal in it at all, which is good for lightning immunity and bad for finding the thing again. Underground locating works by putting an alternating current signal onto a metallic conductor and detecting the field it radiates. No metal, no signal, no locate.

## Tracer wire

The fix is to bury a conductor alongside the duct. Tracer wire is typically solid copper or copper-clad steel, insulated with a jacket rated for direct burial, and coloured orange because that is the colour assigned to communications. It is laid in the trench with the duct, pulled in with the duct on a directional drill, or supplied inside the duct wall.

The wire only works if it is continuous and reachable. Every splice must be made with a waterproof connector, and the wire must be brought up at handholes, pedestals and dedicated test stations so a locator can clip the transmitter onto it. In my experience, broken or inaccessible tracer wire is found at the worst moment, after a strike.

Armored cable with a steel tape sheath can be located directly through the armor, one reason armored designs persist on rural direct-buried routes.

## Warning tape

Warning tape is a plastic ribbon, orange for communications, printed with a caution message, laid in the backfill about a foot above the duct. Its job is to be seen by an excavator operator before the bucket reaches the cable. Detectable tape has a thin metal foil core so it can also be traced with a locator, which is useful on routes that have no tracer wire. Non-detectable tape is purely a visual warning.

In the United States the American Public Works Association (APWA) uniform colour code is used for both buried markings and surface paint.

| Colour | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Red | Electric power |
| Yellow | Gas, oil, steam |
| Orange | Communications, including fiber |
| Blue | Potable water |
| Green | Sewer and drains |
| White | Proposed excavation |
| Pink | Temporary survey marks |

## Markers and one-call

Electronic marker balls, passive resonant devices tuned to a frequency assigned to communications, are dropped in the trench at splice points, bends and crossings so those spots can be pinpointed without tracing the whole route. Above ground, marker posts at road crossings and changes of direction warn anyone with a shovel.

Finally, the whole system depends on people asking. In the United States, calling 811 before digging triggers every utility to mark its plant. Australia has the same service, Before You Dig Australia, formerly Dial Before You Dig. India does not yet have a single national one-call system, so operators rely heavily on marker posts at regular intervals, accurate route records and physical patrols.

## Final thoughts

A buried fiber route is only as safe as the next person's ability to find it. Specify the tracer wire, the access points, the tape and the markers on the same drawing as the cable, and check them at acceptance. What is the strangest way you have seen a buried cable found, or missed?
