A quiet residential street the week before a fiber build starts tells you a lot if you know how to read it. Small flags sprout from the grass strip. Lines of yellow, red and orange spray paint run along the sidewalk, with arrows and letters that look like code. Nobody has dug yet, but the ground has been mapped.

Those marks are the visible end of the United States one-call system, reached by dialing 811. In my experience it is the single most effective safety process in underground construction, and it costs the excavator nothing but a few days of patience.

## How the one-call system works

Before any mechanical excavation, the contractor contacts the state one-call center by phone or online and opens a locate ticket. The ticket describes the dig site, the type of work and the start date. The center notifies every member utility with facilities in that area. Each utility visits the site and marks the approximate horizontal position of its buried lines with paint and flags.

Each state law sets the required notice period and the tolerance zone, a band either side of the mark inside which only careful hand digging or vacuum excavation is allowed. Notice periods are usually a few business days. Tickets also expire, so a long build needs refreshed locates as work moves down the street.

The utility marks only what it owns. Private lines on the customer side of the meter, such as a gas line to a backyard grill or an irrigation feed, are usually not covered.

## What the paint colors mean

The colors follow the American Public Works Association (APWA) uniform color code, which is used nationwide.

| Color | What it marks |
|---|---|
| Red | Electric power lines and conduits |
| Yellow | Gas, oil, steam and other flammable materials |
| Orange | Communications, cable TV, fiber and signal lines |
| Blue | Potable (drinking) water |
| Green | Sewers and drain lines |
| Purple | Reclaimed water and irrigation |
| Pink | Temporary survey markings |
| White | Proposed excavation route |

White paint is the excavator's job, not the locator's. Painting the intended route in white before calling 811 tells the locator exactly where to look.

## Where 811 falls short

Locates mark position, not depth. A conduit may be shallower than drawings suggest because the grade changed when a driveway was rebuilt. Marks also fade, get mowed over or wash away before the crew arrives. I treat a locate as a starting point and expect the crew to pothole, meaning expose the line by hand or vacuum, wherever the bore path crosses a marked utility.

The system also depends on utility records. Abandoned cables and unmapped service drops appear on nobody's map, so a careful crew still watches for warning tape, changes in backfill and the smell of gas.

Australia runs a comparable service, Before You Dig Australia, which was long known as Dial Before You Dig. In India there is no single national one-call number. Designers coordinate directly with municipal bodies and the owners of each utility, and trial pits before trenching are standard practice.

## Final thoughts

The 811 system works because it makes the cheapest step, a phone call, the mandatory first step. The strikes I hear about almost always trace back to someone skipping that call, letting a ticket expire or digging inside the tolerance zone with a machine.
