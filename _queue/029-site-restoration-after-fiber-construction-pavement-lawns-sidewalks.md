Nobody calls the city to say the fiber works. They call to say the sidewalk panel is cracked, the lawn has a brown stripe through it, or the patch in the road has sunk enough to rattle their car. In my experience, the public judges a fiber build almost entirely by what the ground looks like a month after the crew has left.

Restoration is also where the money leaks out. A trench that was cheap to dig becomes expensive when it has to be repaired twice.

## Soft surfaces: lawns and verges

Grass is the most common surface and the one most often done badly. The problem is settlement. Backfill that is not compacted in layers will sink over the following months, leaving a visible trough along the route. Topsoil that was mixed with the subsoil during excavation will not grow grass. Good practice is simple: keep the turf and topsoil separate from the spoil, compact the backfill in lifts, replace the topsoil, and either relay the original turf or seed and cover it. Then water it, or arrange for the homeowner to. A bore that only disturbed small pits at each end is much easier to put right than a plowed or trenched line.

## Hard surfaces: asphalt and concrete

Pavement repair is governed by the permit, and the permit usually says more than people read. Most road authorities require saw-cut edges, backfill with a specified material compacted in layers, a base course, and a patch that is flush with the surrounding surface. Many require the surface cut to be wider than the trench below it, sometimes called a T-cut, so the patch sits on undisturbed pavement rather than on the backfill. Concrete sidewalks are generally replaced panel by panel, joint to joint, not patched in the middle of a panel.

Micro-trenches have their own rules: the slot is filled with a grout or sealant rather than asphalt, and the quality of that seal decides whether the road authority will ever let you do it again.

## Closing out the permit

A restoration is not finished when the crew thinks it looks fine. It is finished when the owner of the surface says so. The closeout steps I expect on any build are:

1. Walk the route with the inspector, comparing before and after photos taken from the same spots.
2. Fix the punch list, including anything the homeowners have reported, before asking for sign-off.
3. Submit the closeout package: photos, compaction or material records if required, and the as-built route.
4. Note the warranty or maintenance period in the project file, because the authority can call you back for settlement within it.
5. Release the permit bond or deposit only after that period has passed.

## How this differs by country

In the United States, the contractor restores the surface to the permit specification and the city inspects it; a maintenance bond often covers later settlement. In India, the municipality usually collects a reinstatement charge up front and may do the road repair itself, so the operator's control over quality is limited and the timing can be slow. In Australia, local councils publish reinstatement specifications and often inspect to them closely, with the contractor doing the work.
