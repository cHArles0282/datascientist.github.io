A construction yard tells you a lot about a program. When cable drums and conduit pallets start stacking up with nowhere to go, the field has outrun the office. The crews are ready, the materials are ready, and the drawings are not. I have seen this more than once, and the cause is almost always the same: nobody matched the design team to the job intake.

Capacity planning is the unglamorous work of making sure that does not happen.

## Start with the intake

Intake is the number of jobs the design team receives in a month, broken down by type. A drop design, a small reroute, a new distribution area and a full feeder route are not the same job, and counting them as one number hides the real workload.

In my experience, the intake is rarely steady. Fiber-to-the-home (FTTH) programs release work in waves tied to funding, permitting and construction seasons. In the US, the spring and summer build season pulls design demand forward into winter. In India, the monsoon shifts construction and therefore design. In Australia, release areas for the National Broadband Network (NBN) tend to arrive in defined tranches. A good plan looks at the next three to six months, not just this month.

## Measure the throughput

Throughput is how many hours a job of each type actually takes, including review and rework. The only reliable way to get this is to track it. Timesheets by job type for a few months give a far better number than gut feel, which is almost always too optimistic because it forgets rework and waiting time.

Available hours per drafter are also lower than they look. A full-time month loses hours to leave, training, meetings, system downtime and the queries that come with every job. Planning on every hour being productive is the fastest way to miss the plan.

## Build the match

Once you have intake and throughput, the arithmetic is simple. Here is the sequence I use:

1. List each job type and the expected count for the month.
2. Multiply each count by the measured hours per job, including review.
3. Add the totals to get demand hours.
4. Divide by the realistic available hours per drafter to get the headcount needed.
5. Compare with the current team and note the gap, month by month.
6. Decide how to close the gap: hire, cross-train, shift work between teams, or negotiate intake with the client.

The output is a simple chart of demand hours against capacity hours. When the demand line crosses the capacity line, that is the month the yard starts filling with drums.

## Manage the peaks

The hard part is the peak, not the average month. Hiring for the peak leaves people idle later, and hiring for the average means missed turnaround targets at the peak. The usual answers are a small permanent core, a pool of cross-trained people who can move between drafting and quality checking, and an agreed overflow partner. US and Australian operators often use offshore teams in India for exactly this reason: the time zone difference adds a second shift without local headcount.

Whatever the mix, the plan has to be reviewed monthly against actual intake. A plan built once and left alone is just a spreadsheet.

## Final thoughts

Capacity planning is not complicated, but it demands honest numbers about how long work really takes. Teams that measure their own throughput make better promises and keep more of them. How does your team decide how many designers a program needs?
