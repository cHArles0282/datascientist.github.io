Look inside the cab of a fiber construction truck and you will often find two sets of drawings on the dash. One is a thin set with a city stamp on the cover, folded many times. The other is thick, dog-eared, and covered in pencil notes about footages, splice cases and the handhole that moved because of a water meter. They describe the same route. They are not the same document.

New designers often ask why one route needs two drawing sets. The answer is that they are written for two different readers.

## Who reads each set

The permit drawing is read by a reviewer at a city, county, state transport agency or railroad. That person is responsible for the road, not for the network. They want to know where the facility sits relative to the right-of-way (ROW) line and the pavement, how deep it is, how it will be placed, what it crosses and how the surface will be restored.

The construction drawing is read by the crew foreman, the splicer and the inspector. They want to know what to build, how much material it takes, where every piece goes, and how the fibers connect.

## What each set shows

| Element | Permit drawing | Construction drawing |
|---|---|---|
| ROW line and property lines | Always, clearly labeled | Shown, but secondary |
| Offset from edge of pavement or curb | Yes, dimensioned | Yes |
| Depth of cover and crossing profiles | Yes | Yes |
| Placement method per segment | Yes | Yes, with notes |
| Conduit size and count | Yes, basic | Yes, with innerduct details |
| Cable fiber count and type | Usually not | Yes |
| Splice locations and tray details | No | Yes |
| Handhole and pedestal sizes | Footprint only | Full part specification |
| Slack loops and storage | No | Yes, with lengths |
| Traffic control plan | Yes, often required | Referenced |
| Bill of materials | No | Yes |

Both sets share the same base map, the same alignment and the same key dimensions. If they differ on any of those, something has gone wrong.

## Why they diverge

The permit set is frozen when it is approved. The construction set keeps living. Field conditions change a handhole location, a pole owner refuses an attachment, a customer asks for a different drop point. Each change goes into the construction drawings and, if it affects the permitted alignment, back to the authority as a permit revision.

In my experience, trouble starts when the crew builds from the permit set because it arrived first, or when a permit revision is skipped because the change seemed small. A handhole moved two metres across the ROW line is a small change that becomes a large problem at inspection.

## Practice in different markets

In the United States the two sets are usually distinct, with the permit set simplified into the authority's own format. In India the RoW application is more often a route map with chainages and a schedule of quantities than a full drawing set. Australian practice sits between, with council submissions drawn from the construction set but trimmed to what the reviewer needs.

## Final thoughts

Two sets, two readers, one route. Keep the shared elements identical, keep the permit set simple, and keep the construction set current.

Do you produce separate permit and construction sets, or one set that serves both?
