Walk past an aerial fiber build and you will often hear it before you see it. A lashing machine clicks and whirs as a lineman pulls it along the strand with a rope, wrapping a thin stainless wire around the new cable in a tight spiral. Behind the machine, the cable that was hanging loose a minute ago is now locked to the steel above it.

That steel is the messenger strand, and the spiral is the lashing. Between them they carry most aerial telecom cables in North America.

## The messenger strand carries the load

A fiber cable on its own has very little tensile strength. The messenger strand, usually just called strand, takes that job instead. It is a galvanized steel wire rope, normally seven wires twisted together, tensioned between poles to a designed sag.

Strand is specified by breaking strength. In the United States the common telecom sizes are written as 6.6M and 10M, where M stands for thousands of pounds. At each pole the strand sits in a three-bolt clamp on a thimble-eye bolt through the pole. At the end of a run or at a corner it is dead-ended, and a down guy carries the unbalanced pull to an anchor in the ground.

Because strand is metal, it is bonded and grounded. In the United States the National Electrical Safety Code (NESC) sets out where communication strand must be bonded to the power neutral and grounded.

## How lashing works

Lashing wire is a thin stainless steel wire. The lashing machine rides on the strand with the fiber cable held beneath it, and a spinning head wraps the wire around both cable and strand. Single lashing uses one wire. Double lashing uses two wires spiralling in opposite directions for heavier cables or bundles that already hold several cables.

At each pole the lashing wire is cut and secured with a lashing wire clamp on either side.

Overlashing is a big reason this method survives. When a new fiber cable is added to a route that already has a lashed copper or coaxial cable, the crew lashes the new cable onto the existing bundle. No new strand, no new pole hardware, and often a simpler permit.

## A lashed span, step by step

1. Install pole hardware and place the strand through the clamps.
2. Tension the strand to the designed sag and dead-end it.
3. Bond and ground the strand where the design requires.
4. Pull the fiber cable along the strand.
5. Lash the cable with the machine.
6. Clamp the lashing wire and dress the cable at each pole.
7. Inspect sag, clearances and hardware before the splicers arrive.

## Practice in other markets

In my experience the strand-and-lash method is the default for telecom in the United States, where joint-use rules and existing strand make it practical. In India, self-supporting designs such as figure-8 cable, which has its own steel messenger moulded into the jacket, are far more common because they go up quickly with fewer parts. Australian operators use both approaches depending on the pole owner's rules.

## Final thoughts

The strand-and-lash system is simple, repairable and easy to expand. Understanding it helps a designer read an existing pole line and plan a new attachment that fits.
