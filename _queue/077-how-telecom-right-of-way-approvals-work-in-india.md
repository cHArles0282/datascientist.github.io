Along a two-lane road outside a small Indian town, a crew is cutting a shallow trench by hand and laying a bright orange duct into it. A supervisor keeps a plastic folder with the permission letter and the receipt for restoration charges. If a local official stops the work, that folder is the first thing he reaches for.

Right of way (RoW) in India has moved a long way from the days when every district and every municipal body had its own forms and its own fees. The direction has been towards one set of rules and one online window.

## The legal framework

Telecom RoW in India sits under central law. For many years the governing instrument was the Indian Telegraph Right of Way Rules, issued in 2016 under the old Telegraph Act, with later amendments. The Telecommunications Act of 2023 replaced the Telegraph Act, and new RoW rules were issued under it. The rules apply to both underground infrastructure, such as ducts and cables, and overground infrastructure, such as poles and towers.

The central rules set out application timelines, fee structures and the idea of deemed approval. If the authority does not act on a complete application within the set period, the permission is treated as granted. States are expected to align their own RoW policies with the central rules, and most have done so to varying degrees.

## Who grants permission

The granting authority depends on who controls the land, much as in the United States.

1. Municipal corporations and urban local bodies for roads inside towns and cities.
2. State public works departments for state highways and district roads.
3. The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) for national highways, including crossings.
4. Indian Railways for crossings of railway land.
5. Forest departments where the route passes through notified forest land.
6. Gram panchayats and rural development bodies for village roads.
7. Private owners for private land, by agreement.

## The GatiShakti Sanchar portal

The Department of Telecommunications launched a national online RoW portal, known as the GatiShakti Sanchar portal, so that an applicant can file RoW requests to central and state authorities through one interface. The portal tracks each application, shows its status and records approvals and rejections. States have been brought onto it progressively, so that an applicant need not visit an office to file or follow up.

In my experience, the portal works best when the application is complete on the first attempt. That means a route map with chainages, the length of trench or number of poles, the restoration method and the fee calculation, all matching one another.

## What makes Indian RoW different

A few features stand out compared with US practice. Restoration charges are typically paid up front to the authority, which then restores the road itself, rather than the carrier restoring it under inspection. Fees are often set per kilometre or per pole by rule, rather than negotiated. And the deemed approval mechanism has no real US equivalent, where a silent authority simply means no permit.

Field reality still varies. A central rule does not stop a local engineer asking for an extra document, and designers plan for that. The folder on site is still good practice.

## Final thoughts

Indian RoW is moving from negotiation towards rule-based, portal-driven approval. The designer's job is to make the application so complete that the rules can work as intended.
