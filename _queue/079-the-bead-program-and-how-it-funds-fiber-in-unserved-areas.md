Drive a gravel county road in rural America and you can see where the old network stopped. The copper line on the poles thins out, then ends at a farmhouse. Beyond it there was never a business case for fiber. In the past few years, pedestals have started appearing on roads like that. Many of them trace back to a federal program with a four-letter name.

The Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment program, known as BEAD, is the largest single broadband funding effort the United States has run. This post keeps to the general shape of the program, because the details have changed more than once.

## Where the money comes from

BEAD was created by the federal infrastructure law, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act. It is administered by the National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA), an agency within the Department of Commerce. The money does not go straight to carriers. It is allocated to each state and territory, and each state runs its own grant program under NTIA rules.

That two-layer structure matters. The rules a designer actually deals with come from the state broadband office, within the frame set by NTIA.

## Who is eligible to be served

Eligibility is decided location by location, using the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) national broadband map. Each home or business is classified by the best service available to it.

| Category | Definition | BEAD priority |
|---|---|---|
| Unserved | No reliable service at 25 Mbps down and 3 Mbps up | First |
| Underserved | Above the unserved level but below 100 Mbps down and 20 Mbps up | Second |
| Served | Reliable service at 100 Mbps down and 20 Mbps up or better | Not eligible |

Before funds are awarded, states run a challenge process where providers, local governments and others can dispute the map. A location an incumbent claims to serve, but does not, can be challenged into eligibility.

## How projects are awarded

States group eligible locations into project areas and invite providers to bid. The original rules strongly preferred fiber as the technology of choice. The program was later revised to be more technology-neutral, giving more weight to cost and allowing fixed wireless and satellite where fiber is expensive to reach. Providers compete on cost to the program, speed and reliability, and the state picks a winner for each area.

Winners take on obligations. Build to every location in the area, meet service and pricing commitments, and report progress. Because the funding is federal, the environmental and historic preservation reviews described in an earlier post apply, as do domestic sourcing rules for materials.

## What it means for designers

A BEAD design is driven by the location list. Every eligible address must be passed and able to be connected. Route choices follow the addresses, not the easiest road. Documentation is heavy, because every location counts toward a milestone. And permitting along rural state and county roads, plus rail and environmental approvals, becomes the main schedule risk. In my experience, rural builds like this live or die on how early the permit applications go in.

## Final thoughts

BEAD is the reason fiber is reaching roads that private investment never would. It is also a program whose rules have shifted over time, so check the current state guidance before relying on anything written here.
