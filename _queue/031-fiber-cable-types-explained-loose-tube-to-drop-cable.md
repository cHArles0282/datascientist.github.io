Look into an open handhole on a new build and you will often see three or four very different cables coming in. One is thick, black and stiff. Another is thin and flat with a small steel messenger. Inside the cabinet nearby there is a yellow cord that bends like a shoelace. All of them carry glass fiber, but each is built for a different job.

In my experience, choosing the wrong cable construction causes more rework than choosing the wrong fiber count.

## Loose tube cable

Loose tube is the workhorse of outside plant (OSP), the network that runs outdoors between cabinets, poles and homes. The fibers sit loosely inside small plastic buffer tubes, usually twelve per tube. The tubes are filled with gel or dry water-blocking yarn and are stranded around a central strength member.

Because the fibers float, the cable can expand and contract with temperature without stressing the glass. That is why loose tube is the default for duct, direct burial and aerial routes in the US, Australia and India alike.

## Ribbon cable

Ribbon cable bonds twelve fibers side by side into a flat strip, and several ribbons are stacked inside a tube or central core. The advantage is density. A ribbon cable can pack hundreds or thousands of fibers into a jacket not much larger than a modest loose tube cable.

It also splices faster, twelve fibers at a time with a mass fusion splicer. The trade-off is that single fibers are harder to break out, so ribbon tends to live in backbone and feeder segments rather than in distribution to homes.

## Tight-buffered cable

Tight-buffered cable is the indoor cousin. Each fiber has a plastic coating applied directly over the glass, bringing it up to about 900 microns. That makes the fiber strong enough to terminate on a connector without a splice, and flexible enough to route through trays and risers.

It has no water blocking, so it belongs indoors, in data centers and in patch cords. US jackets carry plenum and riser fire ratings; Australia and India have their own building wiring rules, so check the local code.

## Armored and drop cables

Armor is an extra layer, not a separate fiber type. A corrugated steel tape sits under the outer jacket to resist rodents and crushing. For direct burial and rocky ground I have found armored loose tube to be the sensible choice. The cost is weight, a larger bend radius and the need to bond and ground the metal.

Drop cable is the last link to a home or business. It carries one to twelve fibers, is small enough to staple to a wall or lash to a span, and comes in flat, round and figure-8 self-supporting forms. Most modern drops use bend-insensitive fiber so a tight corner at the house does not cause loss.

| Cable type | Where it is used | Key feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loose tube | Duct, buried and aerial OSP | Fibers float in gel or dry tubes |
| Ribbon | High-count backbone and feeder | 12 fibers bonded flat, mass splicing |
| Tight-buffered | Indoor, risers, patch cords | 900 micron coating, direct termination |
| Armored | Direct burial, rodent areas | Steel tape under the jacket |
| Drop | Pole or pedestal to premises | Small, flexible, often self-supporting |

## Final thoughts

Most networks use several of these families on one route, and the handoffs between them are where closures and terminals live. Once you can recognize each type on sight, construction drawings become much easier to read.
