A crew is set up on a footpath with a compressor on a trailer and a machine about the size of a sewing machine clamped to a tube no thicker than a garden hose. There is no winch and no pull rope. A technician feeds a thin cable into the machine, air hisses, and the cable starts moving. Twenty minutes later a colleague radios from a pit several hundred metres away to say it has arrived.

That is air-blown fiber in a microduct, and once you have seen it work you wonder why anyone still pulls cable with a rope.

## What a microduct is

A microduct is a small plastic tube, usually high-density polyethylene (HDPE), with an outside diameter from about 5 mm up to around 16 mm. Sizes are written as outside over inside diameter, so 7/5.5 mm, 10/8 mm, 12/10 mm and 14/10 mm are all common. Microducts come singly or as bundles of several tubes inside an outer sheath. Thick-wall versions are direct buried or micro-trenched; thin-wall versions are installed inside a conventional conduit to subdivide it.

The cables inside are microcables, built with the same fibers as any outdoor cable but with a much thinner jacket, so a cable carrying dozens or even a few hundred fibers fits an 8 mm bore.

## How blowing works

A blowing machine has a pair of drive wheels or belts that push the cable into the duct, and a sealed head through which compressed air enters. The air flows along the duct past the cable and the viscous drag along the cable's whole length carries it forward. Unlike a rope pull, where all the force concentrates at the pulling eye and multiplies at every bend, the force is spread along the cable. That is why blowing distances of many hundreds of metres, and over a kilometre on a straight route, are routine. For longer routes, you blow from the middle in both directions or pick up the cable at an intermediate pit and blow on.

## Why it speeds up deployment

1. Install the duct bundle once, then blow cables only where and when customers sign up. The civil work, which is most of the cost, is done one time.
2. A blow is far quicker than a pull and needs fewer intermediate access points, so fewer pits and smaller ones.
3. Micro-trenching or a narrow slot cut in the road surface is enough for a bundle, which is easier to get permitted in a city than a full trench.
4. Upgrades are simple. A cable can be blown out and a higher-count one blown in through the same tube.

## What has to be right

Blowing is unforgiving of a bad duct. Every tube must be clean, dry, continuous and sealed with end caps from the day it is installed. Before a blow, the crew pressure-tests the tube and sends a small sponge or test ball through to prove it. Tight bends, crushed sections and water stop a cable dead.

Microduct has been standard in Europe for years and is widely used in Australia and India, where it suits dense urban areas with restricted road opening. In the United States it is now common in greenfield fiber to the home (FTTH) builds, with conventional conduit still dominant elsewhere.

## Final thoughts

Microduct turns the fiber build into two separate jobs: civils once, cable many times. For a network that grows in steps, that changes how the money is spent. Have you worked on a project where blowing replaced pulling, and what surprised you most?
