Picture a splicer in a bucket truck, closure open, holding a splice diagram that calls for a 24-fiber cable. The cable in his hand has twelve. He cannot finish the splice, the truck cannot move to the next closure, and somebody in an office is about to get a phone call.

That mismatch was probably a two-minute fix at the design desk. By the time it reached the pole it had become a half-day of lost crew time and a change order.

## What first-pass acceptance means

First-pass acceptance is the share of design packages that the client, or the next review stage, accepts without sending them back. If a team submits ten jobs and three come back with corrections, the first-pass rate for that batch is seventy percent. Some clients call it first-time-right or first-pass yield.

A low rate means the team is working every job twice. A high rate means the checking happens where it is cheapest, before submission.

## Where rework comes from

In my experience, most rework in outside plant (OSP) design falls into a few categories, and they look much the same on every project.

| Defect category | Typical example | Usual root cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Field data | Pole missing from drawing, wrong span length | Incomplete or old survey |
| Fiber math | Splitter ports exceed drops served, wrong cable count | No check of counts against the splice plan |
| Standards | Wrong symbol, wrong layer, wrong label format | Designer not trained on the client standard |
| Constructability | Hand hole in a driveway, crossing without clearance | Design done from the map, not the photos |
| Documentation | BOM does not match drawing, missing sheet | Package assembled in a hurry |

Very few of these are hard engineering problems. They are attention and process problems, and process problems can be fixed.

## Why rework costs more than it looks

A correction at the design desk costs minutes. The same correction after permitting means a resubmission, and some agencies restart their review clock. After construction has started it means idle crews, extra materials and sometimes a second permit. After service is live it can mean an outage to fix.

The pattern holds in the US, Australia and India. What changes is the price of each step. In a US aerial build, the expensive stage is usually make-ready and crew time. In an Australian underground build through existing pits, it is often access and traffic management. In India, where Right of Way (RoW) permissions can take longer than the build itself, redoing a permit is the step everyone fears.

## How to raise the first-pass rate

A few habits make the biggest difference. Use a written checklist that mirrors the client's review checklist, so the designer checks what the reviewer will check. Reconcile fiber counts, splitter ports and drop counts in a simple spreadsheet before the drawing is finalised. Have a second person review every package, and rotate reviewers so nobody checks only their own work. Finally, log every returned job by defect category and review the log monthly; it turns rework into a training plan.

## Final thoughts
