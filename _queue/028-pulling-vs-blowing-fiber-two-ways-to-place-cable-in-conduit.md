Two handholes, a block apart, both open. At one, a crew has a winch on a tripod and a rope disappearing into a conduit, with someone watching a tension gauge. At the other, on another day, a compressor is running and a small machine clamped to a bright orange duct feeds cable into it with a steady hiss. Both crews are doing the same job: getting fiber from one end of a conduit to the other.

Pulling and blowing are the two ways to do it, and they are not interchangeable.

## Pulling

Pulling is the older method. A pull rope or tape is installed in the conduit first, often blown in with a foam carrier or placed when the conduit was laid. The cable is attached to the rope through a pulling eye and a swivel so it does not twist, and a winch at the far end takes up the rope while the cable is fed in at the near end, with lubricant applied as it enters.

The limiting factor is tension. Every bend in the route multiplies the force needed, and that force is concentrated at the cable's strength members. Each cable has a maximum pulling tension published by its manufacturer, and exceeding it can stretch the fibers inside without visible damage. On longer or bendier routes the crew pulls in stages, figure-eighting the cable on the ground at intermediate points.

## Blowing

Blowing, also called jetting, pushes the cable into the duct with a pair of drive wheels while a compressor forces air down the duct at high speed. The moving air drags along the whole length of the cable, so the force is spread out rather than concentrated at the front. The cable effectively floats, with little friction at bends.

This is why blowing can reach long distances in one setup and why it is the standard method for microducts, which are too small for a rope. The duct has to be clean, dry, and pressure-tight. A kinked or crushed duct that would let a rope through will stop a blow dead.

## Choosing between them

| Factor | Pulling | Blowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duct type | Conventional conduit | Microduct or lubricated conduit |
| Route with many bends | Poor | Good |
| Force on cable | Concentrated at front, high | Distributed, low |
| Equipment | Winch, rope, swivel, lubricant | Blowing head, compressor |
| Clean, sealed duct | Helpful | Essential |
| Removing cable later | Difficult | Often possible |

Practice differs by market. In India, blowing into permanently lubricated high-density polyethylene (PLB HDPE) duct is standard for long-haul and access routes, with 40/33 mm the most common size. In the United States, pulling into larger conduit is still common in older plant, while new fiber-to-the-home (FTTH) builds lean heavily on microduct and blowing. Australian builds use both, often blowing into existing duct originally built for copper.

## What damages cable

Most installation damage comes from the same few mistakes regardless of method: exceeding the bend radius at the entry point, ignoring the tension or speed limit, and running cable through a duct full of water or grit. A cable pulled too hard or blown too fast can test fine on the day and fail years later.

## Final thoughts

I think of pulling as the method you use when you have to and blowing as the one you design for. If you have both options, which one does your crew reach for first, and why?
