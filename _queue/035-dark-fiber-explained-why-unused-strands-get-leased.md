Lift the lid on a manhole along a major road and you may find a 288-fiber cable where only a couple of buffer tubes have ever been spliced. The rest sit coiled in the closure, unlit and waiting. That idle glass is dark fiber, and it can be one of the most valuable things an operator owns.

In my experience, new designers are surprised by how much of a network is dark on day one, and by how often that spare capacity becomes a product in its own right.

## What dark fiber actually is

Fiber is "lit" when electronics at each end are sending light through it. It is "dark" when no equipment is attached. The glass is identical; the only difference is whether transceivers are on the ends.

Operators install far more fibers than they need because the cost of a trench, duct or aerial strand dwarfs the cost of the glass inside. Adding fibers before the cable goes in is cheap; adding a second cable later is a second construction project. So routes are built with spare strands, and those strands can be sold.

## Why someone leases dark fiber

The customer for dark fiber is usually another network, not a home or a small business. Typical buyers are mobile operators needing tower backhaul, data center companies linking sites across a city, enterprises and universities that run their own equipment, and smaller internet providers reaching a new area without digging.

What they get is control. On dark fiber the customer chooses the electronics, the wavelength plan and the capacity. They can upgrade from 10 Gb/s to 400 Gb/s by swapping optics without telling the fiber owner. On a lit service the provider sells fixed bandwidth and manages the equipment.

## How it is sold

Dark fiber changes hands in a few ways, and the vocabulary matters when you read a route agreement.

| Model | Term | Who owns the glass | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short-term lease | Months to a few years | Fiber owner | Trials, temporary links |
| IRU | Long term, often ten to twenty years | Fiber owner, but rights sit with the buyer | Carrier backhaul, data center links |
| Own build | Permanent | The builder | Core routes, campus networks |

An IRU, or indefeasible right of use, is the one that puzzles most people. It is a long-term contract, usually paid largely up front, that gives the buyer exclusive rights to specific strands for the life of the agreement. "Indefeasible" means the right cannot be cancelled or taken back, short of the buyer failing to pay. An annual maintenance fee usually covers repairs and relocations. Accountants often treat an IRU more like an asset purchase than a rental.

## Regional differences

In the US, dark fiber is largely a commercial matter between private parties. In India, leasing dark fiber is tied to licensing. Infrastructure providers that register with the government can lease fiber and ducts to licensed operators, which has supported the growth of tower and backhaul companies. In Australia, dark fiber is common in metro and campus networks, while the national access network is sold mainly as lit wholesale services.

## Final thoughts

For a designer, dark fiber is a reminder to think past the first customer. The spare tubes you draw today may be the asset that pays for the route tomorrow. Plan the splice points, the slack and the records so those strands can be handed over cleanly when the time comes.
