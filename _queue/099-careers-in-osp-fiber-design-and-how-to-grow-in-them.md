Most people in this field can remember the first time they stood under a utility pole and tried to work out what everything on it was. Power at the top. A thick black cable with a messenger strand. A smaller one with a grey closure hanging off it. A coil of slack lashed to the strand. Nothing on it was labelled, and nothing in the degree had explained it.

That moment is roughly where a career in outside plant (OSP) design begins. Here is how the roles fit together and how people move up.

## The roles

OSP design is a team sport, and the team is wider than most students expect.

| Role | What they do | Typical background |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Field surveyor | Collects pole data, photos and measurements | Any technical trade; good observation |
| CAD drafter | Draws the design to the client standard | Diploma or degree; drafting software skills |
| OSP designer | Chooses routes, sizes cables, plans splices | Engineering degree or promoted drafter |
| QC reviewer | Checks packages against the standard | Experienced designer |
| Permitting specialist | Prepares permit and pole attachment applications | Admin or engineering; local knowledge |
| Project manager | Plans capacity, tracks turnaround, manages the client | Senior designer or PM background |

In India, most entry-level hiring is into drafting and design for offshore programs. In the US and Australia the same roles exist, but survey and permitting roles are more common because they have to be done locally.

## The skills that matter

Drafting software is the entry ticket. Most OSP work is done in computer-aided design (CAD) tools or in geographic information system (GIS) platforms, and many clients use both. Learning one well and the other adequately is enough to start.

Beyond the software, three skills separate good designers from average ones. The first is fiber arithmetic: cable counts, splitter ratios, splice allocation and loss budgets. The second is reading the field: looking at a pole photo and seeing clearance problems, obstructions and the right place for a closure. The third is attention to standards, because a technically sound design drawn in the wrong format is still a returned design.

Communication rounds it out. Designers who can write a clear query to a surveyor or a client save everyone a day.

## Tools and certifications

Common tools include general CAD packages, GIS software, spreadsheets for fiber counts and bills of materials, and street-level imagery for desktop checks. Certifications from bodies such as the Fiber Optic Association help, but in my experience employers weigh a portfolio of clean, accepted work more heavily than a certificate.

## Growth paths

The common path is surveyor or drafter, then designer, then senior designer or quality lead, then design manager or project manager. Some branch into GIS and data management, some into construction supervision, and some into client-side roles at an operator. Moving between countries is also realistic. The physics and most of the symbols are the same, and someone who has designed US aerial plant from India can adapt to Australian underground plant in a few months.

## Final thoughts

OSP design is a practical, steady career with more variety than it looks from the outside, and demand for fiber is not going away. The engineers who grow fastest stay curious about what is actually on the pole. What was the first thing that confused you on a job site, and how did you learn it?
