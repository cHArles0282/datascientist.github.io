Walk along the gutter line of a city street after a micro-trenching crew has been through and you might miss it entirely. All that is left is a thin dark line in the asphalt, a finger's width across, running for blocks and turning neatly at each driveway. Under that line is a bundle of microducts, and inside those, fiber serving the whole street.

Micro-trenching gets a lot of attention because it promises speed. It also gets a lot of pushback from road owners. Both reactions are reasonable.

## What micro-trenching is

A micro-trench is a slot cut into the road surface with a specialized saw. It is only a few centimeters wide and typically stays within the upper pavement layers rather than going deep into the road base. The crew drops microducts into the slot, sometimes several bundled together, then seals the cut with a flowable grout or a sealant designed to bond to the asphalt.

The fiber itself is usually a microcable, a small-diameter cable built to be blown into a microduct. Because the slot is so narrow, there is no room for conventional conduit or splice closures in the trench; those sit in small pits or at the roadside.

## Why it is fast

The saw, the duct placement, and the reinstatement happen in one pass behind a single machine. There is no spoil to haul away beyond a slurry, no compaction of backfill, and no separate paving crew. Driveway crossings do not need to be plated. On a clear run with good asphalt, a crew can cover far more distance in a day than open trenching allows.

## Where it goes wrong

Shallow depth is the whole point and also the whole risk. The table below shows the trade-offs as I see them against the two conventional methods.

| Factor | Micro-trenching | Open trench | Directional drilling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surface disruption | Very low | High | Low |
| Daily production | High | Low to medium | Medium |
| Depth of cover | Shallow | Deep | Deep |
| Exposure to road resurfacing | High | Low | Very low |
| Exposure to other utility cuts | High | Medium | Low |
| Restoration cost | Low | High | Low |

Road milling and resurfacing is the big one. A standard mill can remove the top layer of asphalt, and the fiber with it. Utility crews opening the road for a water or gas repair can slice through a micro-trench without ever seeing it. In cold climates, water entering a poorly sealed cut and freezing will open the joint over time. For these reasons many road authorities refuse micro-trenching outright, or restrict it to the gutter line with strict sealing specifications.

## Approvals and practice by country

In the United States, acceptance depends on the city or county, and the same method can be welcomed in one jurisdiction and banned in the next. India has actively promoted micro-trenching through its right-of-way rules as a way to speed up urban fiber, and it is widely used there. In Australia it has been used selectively, usually where existing duct is full and the road owner agrees.

## Final thoughts

Micro-trenching is a genuine tool, not a gimmick, but it moves risk from construction day to the years that follow. It makes sense where the road owner understands that trade and the network is designed with repair in mind. Has your local road authority allowed micro-trenching, and under what conditions?
