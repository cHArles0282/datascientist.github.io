Stand on almost any suburban street in the United States and look up. The wooden pole on the corner is carrying more than most people realise. Power conductors sit at the top on a crossarm, a transformer hangs below them, and further down a bundle of black cables runs along a steel strand, and somewhere in that bundle is probably a fiber cable.

Before I design a single span of aerial fiber, I want to know what that pole is, how tall it is, what it is made of, and who owns it.

## What a pole class means

In the United States, wood poles are specified under ANSI O5.1, the American National Standards Institute standard for wood poles. Each pole has a length and a class. The class is a strength rating set by the minimum circumference at the top and at a point near the ground line. A lower class number means a thicker, stronger pole. Most distribution poles I see on telecom work are Class 4 or Class 5.

| Class | Minimum top circumference | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 27 in | Heavy junction or crossing poles |
| 2 | 25 in | Heavy distribution, corners |
| 3 | 23 in | Distribution with transformers |
| 4 | 21 in | Common distribution pole |
| 5 | 19 in | Light distribution, communication-only lines |

## Heights and setting depth

Distribution poles are commonly 35, 40 or 45 feet long, but not all of that is above ground. A widely used rule of thumb is that the setting depth equals ten percent of the pole length plus two feet. A 40-foot pole therefore has about six feet in the ground and 34 feet of usable height. Forget that and your clearances will not work.

## Materials around the world

Wood dominates in North America. Southern yellow pine, Douglas fir and western red cedar are the usual species, pressure-treated with preservatives such as CCA (chromated copper arsenate) to slow decay. Steel, concrete and fiberglass composite poles are used where wood is impractical or fire risk is high.

In India most distribution poles are PCC (pre-stressed cement concrete) or steel tubular poles, and wood is rare. In Australia hardwood timber was traditional, but steel and concrete are now common, partly because of termites and bushfire exposure.

## Who owns the pole

Ownership decides who you ask for permission and who you pay. In the United States a pole usually belongs to the electric utility or the incumbent telephone company, and sometimes the two own poles jointly along one line. The owner sets the attachment rules, runs the application process and charges an annual rental per pole. A small metal tag near eye level normally identifies the owner and the pole number.

In India, poles are mostly owned by state electricity distribution companies or municipal bodies, and telecom operators attach under right-of-way permissions. In Australia the electricity distributors own most poles.

## Final thoughts

A pole is a structural element, not just a convenient place to hang cable. Class, height, material and owner each change what you are allowed to do and what the pole can safely carry. Get those four facts into your survey notes early and the rest of the aerial design becomes far more predictable.
