Three streets, three countries. In a Texas suburb, a crew lashes fiber to a strand on wooden poles while traffic rolls past. In a Melbourne suburb, a pit lid is lifted and a sub-duct is rodded through a concrete duct laid decades ago. In an Indian city, bundles of cable run along concrete electricity poles above shopfronts, and the nearest closure hangs three metres above a fruit stall. All three are fiber-to-the-home (FTTH) builds. They look nothing alike.

Having worked on all three, I am often asked what is different. The more interesting answer is what is not.

## What is different

| Aspect | United States | Australia | India |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Typical plant | Aerial on utility poles, some underground | Underground in existing pits and ducts | Overhead on electricity poles plus roadside underground |
| Units | Feet | Metres | Metres |
| Key permission | Municipal permit and pole attachment agreement | Duct access and council permit | Right of Way (RoW) from the local authority |
| Main schedule risk | Make-ready on poles | Blocked or full ducts | RoW approval time |
| Network model | Private operators compete | Government wholesaler, retailers sell | Private operators compete |

Pole ownership shapes the US more than anything else. Most poles belong to a power utility or an incumbent telephone company, and attaching to them means an application, a survey and make-ready work to create clearance. Australia's National Broadband Network (NBN) runs mostly through ducts, so the daily problem is whether a duct is blocked, not whether a pole has room. In India, the dominant problem is permission. Right of Way rules vary by state and city, and the time to get approval can exceed the time to build.

## What is the same

The physics does not change. A 1310 nanometre or 1550 nanometre signal through a 32-way splitter loses the same power in Dallas, Melbourne and Mumbai, and the loss budget has to close in all three. Splice closures still need slack. Cable still needs its minimum bend radius. The same twelve-colour fiber sequence turns up on cables in all three countries far more often than not.

The process is the same too. Survey, design, permit, build, test, record. A missing as-built hurts the next project as much in India as in the US. A design that ignores the field photos gets returned in any time zone. And everyone, in every country, forgets the customer at the end of the drop, who does not care which technology the street uses.

## Lessons that travel

A few lessons hold everywhere. Learn the permission system first: whoever controls the pole, the duct or the road controls the schedule. Fix the units in your templates; feet and metres have caused more silent errors than any splice. Trust photos over maps, because the map is a starting point and the street is the truth. Protect the as-built, the cheapest investment a program makes and the first one cut. And respect local practice: an Indian crew's way of handling a closure on a crowded pole is not wrong, it is adapted.

## Final thoughts

Working across these three countries has made me a better designer in each of them. The US taught me pole discipline, Australia taught me records, and India taught me how to get things done when permission is the hardest part. Which of these differences has surprised you the most?
