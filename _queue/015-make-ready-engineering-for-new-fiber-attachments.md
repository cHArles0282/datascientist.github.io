A fiber survey crew stops at a pole and finds three communication cables stacked within a few inches of each other, the lowest one barely clearing a driveway. There is simply no room for a fourth. The pole is sound, the route is right, but the new cable cannot go up until something moves.

That moving is make-ready. Make-ready engineering is the design work that figures out what has to shift, by how much, who does it and what it will cost, before a new attacher is allowed on the pole.

## Why make-ready exists

Every pole has a fixed amount of usable height, and clearance rules fix where cables may sit within it. In the United States the National Electrical Safety Code (NESC) requires a communication worker safety zone of 40 inches between the lowest power conductor and the highest communication cable, and 12 inches between separate communication attachments at the pole.

Existing attachers rarely placed their cables with future neighbours in mind. So when a new cable arrives, the make-ready engineer must find or create a slot that satisfies every clearance above and below it.

## Simple and complex make-ready

Work in the communication space that only involves moving existing cables up or down on the same pole, without splicing or cutting, is usually treated as simple make-ready.

Complex make-ready covers anything riskier: moving power equipment, relocating a cable that would need splicing, re-hanging service drops to many houses, or replacing the pole because no arrangement will fit. Pole replacement is the expensive case. The owner sets a taller or stronger pole beside the old one, each attacher transfers its cable across, and the old pole is removed last. The period when both stand side by side is known as double wood.

## What a make-ready design contains

1. A pole profile for each pole showing every existing attachment and its measured height.
2. The proposed height of the new cable.
3. The required move for each existing attachment, with its new height.
4. Any guying, bonding or hardware changes caused by the moves.
5. Pole replacements, with the new length and class.
6. An estimate of cost by attacher, since each party moves its own cable and bills the new attacher.

A good design also fixes the order of work. Power moves first, then each communication attacher from the top down, so that nobody is asked to move into space that is still occupied.

## Costs, timelines and the new attacher

The new attacher pays for make-ready. That principle is the same almost everywhere. In the United States the FCC sets time limits for each stage of the process on regulated poles, which gives the attacher some leverage when existing attachers are slow.

In India and Australia the idea is the same but the process is less formal. Rearrangement is often negotiated pole by pole with the electricity distributor, and in India it is common to find cables that were never permitted at all, which complicates every decision.

## Final thoughts

Make-ready is where aerial fiber projects gain or lose months. The engineering itself is simple arithmetic about heights, but the coordination between owners and attachers is not. Measure carefully, document everything and plan the order of work as carefully as the heights.
