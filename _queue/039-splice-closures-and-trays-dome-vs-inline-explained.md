Look up at a pole on a fiber route and you may spot a black cylinder strapped to the strand, a little larger than a thermos. Look into a pit on the footpath and you will see its cousin on a bracket with cables looping around it. These are splice closures, and every outdoor fusion splice lives inside one.

In my experience, closures are where good splicing is either preserved for decades or ruined in one wet winter. The splice is tiny; the box around it is what the network depends on.

## What a closure has to do

A closure has four jobs. It keeps water and dirt away from bare fiber. It clamps the cable sheath so no pulling force reaches the glass. It bonds and grounds any metallic armor or strength member. And it organizes the fibers so a technician can find one splice years later without disturbing the rest.

## Dome vs inline

The two main families are named for how the cables enter.

| Feature | Dome closure | Inline closure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Vertical cylinder, removable dome | Horizontal, splits lengthwise |
| Cable entry | All cables through one base | Cables enter both ends |
| Typical location | Pits, handholes, manholes, pole mount | Aerial strand, direct burial, cabinets |
| Re-entry | Lift the dome | Open the lid |
| Branching | Easy, many base ports | Limited by end ports |

A dome closure is the usual choice underground. Every cable comes through a sealed base and the trays stack upward inside the dome. Adding a branch later means opening one more port, which is why domes are common at branch points in the US, in Australian pits and in Indian chambers alike.

An inline closure suits a cable passing straight through, such as a reel-end joint on an aerial span. The cable enters one end and leaves the other, so the closure hangs on the strand with little extra slack, which is why aerial networks in the US and India use them widely.

## Trays and how fibers are organized

Inside either type, the work happens on trays. A tray is a flat plastic plate with a holder for twelve or twenty-four splice sleeves and a routing path that keeps every fiber above its minimum bend radius. Trays stack on a hinge so each lifts away to expose the one beneath.

Most crews put one buffer tube, or one ribbon, per tray, in color order from the bottom up. Incoming fibers are routed in on one side, spliced in the holder and routed out to the outgoing tube on the other. A good splicer leaves a loop of slack so the splice can be remade later.

Not every tube is cut. A tube that does not branch here is passed through uncut and stored in the basket at the base. That is an express tube, and it saves twelve splices and twelve small losses at every closure it passes.

## Sealing

Ports are sealed with heat-shrink sleeves, compression grommets or gel blocks. Heat-shrink is reliable but needs a torch and skill; gel seals are quicker to re-enter. The seal is the part most likely to fail if rushed, so it deserves inspection time.

## Final thoughts

A closure is a small building for glass. Choose the shape for the cable path, size it for the trays you will need in ten years, and treat the seal as the most important splice of all.
