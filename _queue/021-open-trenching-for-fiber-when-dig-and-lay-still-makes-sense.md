Drive down almost any street getting fiber for the first time and you will eventually see it: a long, narrow slot in the grass strip between the sidewalk and the curb, steel plates over the driveway crossings, and a small excavator parked at the end of the block. That is open trenching, the method most people picture when they hear "burying cable."

Newer techniques like directional drilling and micro-trenching were invented to replace it, so it is fair to ask whether it still has a place. In my experience it does, and knowing when is part of being a good outside plant (OSP) designer.

## What open trenching actually involves

Open trenching means digging a continuous slot down to the required depth, placing the conduit or cable in the bottom, and filling it back in. The digging might be done with a backhoe, a chain trencher, or by hand near other utilities. A typical job runs in a fixed order:

1. Locate and mark existing utilities, then pothole anything close to the line.
2. Saw-cut hard surfaces so the edges are clean.
3. Excavate to the design depth and width, keeping spoil separate from topsoil or turf.
4. Place bedding sand if the native soil is rocky, then lay the conduit or cable.
5. Add warning tape above the conduit, backfill in lifts, and compact each lift.
6. Restore the surface and record the as-built route and depth.

Cover depth is set by the permit authority and the operator's standards, so there is no universal number. Every step is visible, which makes inspection straightforward.

## Where trenching still wins

Open cut is the right call more often than people expect. It is the natural choice for placing several ducts in a bank, since drilling a wide duct bank is slow and expensive. It works well in greenfield developments where nothing else is in the ground yet and the roads are not built. It is also the safest method in ground crowded with unknown services, because the crew can see what they are digging around.

On rural routes with soft verges, trenching can be quicker than it looks. A chain trencher moves steadily, and there is no pavement to repair.

## Where it hurts

The cost of trenching is mostly in what happens after the conduit goes in. Restoring asphalt and concrete is slow, and the patch is where most complaints come from. Trenching across a driveway means that household cannot park for a day. Many cities will not allow an open cut through a mature tree root zone.

Practice varies by country. In the United States, trenching in the public right-of-way needs a permit and strict reinstatement rules. In India, open trenching is still the dominant urban method, often with manual labor, and reinstatement charges are typically paid to the municipality in advance. Australian builds often reuse existing duct, so trenching is reserved for genuinely new routes.

## Choosing between trench and bore

I treat it as a question of surface, not distance. If the surface is soft and there is room to work, trenching is hard to beat. If it is hard, busy, or belongs to someone who will not let me cut it, I look at drilling. Most projects use a mix, and the design should show which method applies to each segment.

## Final thoughts

Open trenching is not old-fashioned; it is honest. You see everything, you can inspect everything, and the risks are ones you can plan for. What surface conditions have pushed you away from trenching and toward boring?
