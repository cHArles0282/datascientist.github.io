A boring crew is parked on a cul-de-sac at seven in the morning. Rig on the trailer, locates done, traffic cones out. They are waiting for a revised drawing because a hand hole in the plan sits on top of a water meter. Every hour they wait costs money, and the clock on that drawing started the day before.

That is what a design service level agreement (SLA) really measures: how long the people downstream have to wait for the design team.

## What an SLA means in design work

An SLA is a written commitment between the design provider and the client about turnaround time and quality. For outside plant (OSP) design, it usually sets a target number of business days from receipt of a complete job to delivery of an approved package. It often pairs the time target with a quality target, such as a first-pass acceptance rate.

The word complete matters. Most SLAs start the clock only when the required inputs are present: field survey data, photos, address lists and any constraints from the client. A job that arrives with half its survey missing should be returned, not started.

## How turnaround is measured

Turnaround is usually tracked per job type. A single drop design or a small reroute carries a short target. A full distribution area with hundreds of living units carries a much longer one. Many contracts also distinguish between the first submission and revisions, and pause the clock while the client answers a question.

In the US, the pressure often comes from construction crews and permit windows. In India, offshore teams working for US or Australian clients deal with an extra time zone gap, so a one-day target may really mean overnight. In Australia, delivery milestones for the National Broadband Network (NBN) are often tied to premises counts for a release area, so the SLA sits inside a larger program schedule.

## How teams actually meet the target

The teams I have seen hit their targets consistently tend to do the same handful of things:

1. Triage every job on the day it arrives. Check inputs, classify complexity, and reject incomplete jobs before the clock runs.
2. Use standard work. Templates, naming rules and a defined sequence of design steps remove the thinking from routine jobs.
3. Separate design from review. A second person checks the package against a checklist, which catches errors before the client does.
4. Batch similar jobs. Ten drop designs in the same area go faster together than scattered through the week.
5. Track a daily dashboard. Jobs at risk of breaching should be visible to the whole team, not discovered at month end.
6. Protect capacity for rework. A target with zero slack is a target that will be missed the first time a client sends a revision.

## When SLAs go wrong

The usual failures are predictable. The clock starts on incomplete data. Jobs are counted as delivered when they are merely submitted, then bounced back. Or the team hits the time target by cutting the quality review, which just shifts the delay into rework. A good SLA treats time and quality as one measure, not two competing ones.

## Final thoughts

An SLA is only useful if both sides measure it the same way and both sides respect the definition of a complete job. The best agreements I have worked under were short, specific, and reviewed whenever the job mix changed. What turnaround target do you think is realistic for a typical fiber distribution design?
