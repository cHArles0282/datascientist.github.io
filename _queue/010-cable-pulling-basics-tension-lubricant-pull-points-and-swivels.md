A reel trailer is parked over one handhole and a winch truck over another, a few hundred metres down the street. A thin rope runs through the duct between them. A radio crackles: "What are you reading?" "About three hundred." "Keep coming." The cable moves a metre at a time, with a technician at the reel end smearing clear gel onto it as it disappears into the pipe.

Pulling is the moment a cable is most likely to be damaged. The damage rarely shows at the time; it appears as an attenuation step on the test results, or as a broken fiber months later.

## Pulling tension

Every fiber cable has a maximum rated installation tension in its data sheet. For outside plant loose-tube cable a rating of 600 pounds-force, about 2,700 newtons, is the common figure. That strength comes from the central strength member and aramid yarns, not from the fibers, so the pulling force must be attached to those elements.

Tension builds along a pull from friction, and it multiplies at every bend rather than adding. That is why I plan pulls so the bends come early, close to the reel, where the cable is still slack. The minimum bend radius also matters: a common rule is twenty times the cable diameter while under tension and ten times once installed.

## Lubricant

Pulling lubricant is a water-based gel or liquid that cuts the friction between cable jacket and duct wall by a large margin. It is applied at the feed end and worked into the duct ahead of the cable, continuously, not once at the start. It must be compatible with the cable jacket and the duct material. Grease, oil and dish soap are not substitutes; they attack jackets, dry hard, or wash away.

## Planning the pull

1. Walk the route and list every handhole, sweep and change of direction between the reel and the winch.
2. Set the reel at the end nearest the bends.
3. Confirm the duct is clear by pulling a mandrel and a swab through first, then install a measured pull tape rather than a round rope, which can saw into duct bends.
4. For a route longer than one safe pull, pull to an intermediate handhole, lay the cable out on the ground in a figure-eight so it cannot twist, and feed it onward from there.
5. Keep radio contact between reel, winch and every intermediate point, and stop immediately when tension climbs with no movement.

## Swivels and grips

The pull tape attaches to the cable through either a factory pulling eye fixed to the strength member or a wire mesh grip that tightens over the jacket. Between the tape and the grip goes a swivel, so that twist in the tape under load is not transferred into the cable. A breakaway swivel adds a shear pin rated below the cable's tension limit; if the pin lets go, the pull stops before the cable is hurt. A winch with a tension meter or dynamometer gives the same protection with a number the crew can watch.

Practice varies by region. In India, cable blowing into permanently lubricated high-density polyethylene (HDPE) duct is the default for long routes, with pulling kept for short sections. In Australia the operation is called hauling and the rope a haul rope, but the physics are the same.

## Final thoughts

A good pull is slow, lubricated, measured and boring. The excitement comes from skipping one of those. What is the longest single pull you have seen done safely, and how was it planned?
