Open a footpath pit on an older street and you will often find a thick grey cable cut open inside a splice case. Hundreds of tiny wires fan out of it, each pair twisted together and colour-coded, some of them stained where water got in years ago. A technician with a tone probe is hunting for one pair among six hundred. Beside it runs a slim black fiber cable that will eventually make the whole exercise unnecessary.

Copper twisted pair is the network most of the world's telephones were built on. It is also the network that most operators are now planning to switch off.

## What a twisted pair actually is

A pair is two insulated copper conductors twisted around each other. The twist is the clever part. Noise picked up along the route hits both wires almost equally, and because the signal is carried as the difference between them, that common noise cancels out. Different twist rates in neighbouring pairs reduce crosstalk between them. Pairs are bundled into binder groups of 25, identified by a colour code built from five tip colours and five ring colours, and binder groups are bundled into cables of hundreds or thousands of pairs. Older cables are air-core and kept dry by pressurisation; newer ones are gel-filled. US practice specifies conductors by gauge, commonly 24 and 26 AWG (American wire gauge), while Australia and India use diameter in millimetres.

## How DSL squeezed broadband out of a phone line

The pair was designed for a voice signal and 48 volts of DC from the exchange to power the handset. Digital subscriber line (DSL) technology sends high-frequency data on the same pair above the voice band. Each generation used more bandwidth and therefore reached a shorter distance.

| Technology | Typical headline downstream | Useful reach from the equipment |
|---|---|---|
| ADSL2+ | Up to about 24 Mbps | A few kilometres |
| VDSL2 | Up to about 100 Mbps | Around a kilometre or less |
| G.fast | Hundreds of Mbps to gigabit class | A few hundred metres at most |

These are the reasons fiber to the node and fiber to the curb exist: the fiber is brought close enough that the copper only has to carry the last short hop. Vectoring, which cancels crosstalk between pairs in the same cable, pushed VDSL2 a little further. But every DSL variant is fighting physics.

## Why copper is being retired

In my experience the case for retirement is rarely about speed alone. Copper plant is expensive to keep alive. Joints corrode, pits flood, and sheath damage lets water wick along the cable. Copper theft is a real problem in some regions. Exchanges full of ageing voice switches cost power and space. And the skills to maintain it are retiring with the technicians.

Regulators have responded in different ways. In the United States, operators can retire copper after giving customers and the regulator notice. In Australia, NBN disconnects the old copper service a fixed period after a premises is declared fiber-ready. In India, the legacy copper footprint was small relative to the population, and most customers moved straight from mobile to fiber without ever having a copper broadband line.

## Final thoughts

Copper served for more than a century and, with DSL, delivered broadband to hundreds of millions of homes it was never designed to serve. Its retirement is not a failure; it is a technology reaching the end of a very long shift. Does your home still have a working copper phone line?
