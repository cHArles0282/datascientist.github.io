At the corner of a lawn, right on the property line, there is a green plastic post about knee high with a lift-off cover. Half a block away on a concrete pad sits a grey steel box the size of a refrigerator, with a padlock and a vent. Both belong to the same fiber network, and both exist because some things are easier to do above the ground than below it.

Underground access is dry, hidden and safe from vehicles, but it means lifting lids and kneeling in mud. Above-ground enclosures trade some exposure for speed of access. Knowing which to use, and where, is a core part of fiber to the home (FTTH) design.

## Pedestals

A pedestal is a small enclosure, usually polymer, mounted on a stake or a base set into the ground. Cables come up from buried conduit stubs through the open bottom. Inside you will find one of a few things: a splice closure where a distribution cable is branched, a multiport service terminal (MST) with pre-connectorised ports for customer drops, or simply the slack and connections for a handful of drops.

Pedestals are cheap, quick to install and quick to open, so they are the workhorse of residential and rural distribution. The trade-off is that they are visible and vulnerable. Mowers, string trimmers, snow ploughs and reversing cars all find them. I try to place them at property lines, away from driveway sight lines and clear of where a car door will swing.

## Cabinets

A cabinet is a larger enclosure, metal or composite, on a concrete pad or a pole, and it houses something more than splices. In a passive optical network, the typical occupant is the fiber distribution hub (FDH), where feeder fibers meet optical splitters, often 1:32, and the split outputs are patched to distribution fibers on connector panels. That patching is why cabinets exist. A technician can connect a new distribution leg or test a port without cutting anything.

Cabinets are sized by distribution ports, with 288 and 432 port units common. Some cabinets house active electronics, which brings in power, batteries, cooling and a different set of maintenance rules. Every cabinet needs a ground rod, a lock, ventilation that keeps water out, and a plinth high enough to sit above flood and snow levels.

| | Pedestal | Cabinet |
|---|---|---|
| Typical size | Knee to waist height | Waist to chest height, on a pad |
| What is inside | Splice closure, terminal, drop slack | Splitters, cross-connect panels, sometimes electronics |
| Placement | Property line, lot corner | Street corner, easement, near feeder route |
| Weak point | Mowers and vehicles | Vandalism, heat, flooding |

## Regional differences

The same functions sit in different hardware around the world. In India, where much of the distribution is aerial, the common equivalent of a pedestal is a pole-mounted or wall-mounted fiber access terminal (FAT) box, and splitters often live in those boxes rather than in a street cabinet. In Australia, FTTH networks use footpath cabinets for the distribution hub and put terminals in pits, so pedestals are rarer than in the United States.

## Final thoughts

Above-ground enclosures are where most of the day-to-day work on a fiber network happens, so their placement affects every service call for the next twenty years. Put them where a technician can park, stand, and open the door without stepping into traffic. What is the worst place you have seen a pedestal or cabinet installed?
