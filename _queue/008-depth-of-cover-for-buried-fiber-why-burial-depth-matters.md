A landscaping crew is cutting a slot across a front lawn for an irrigation line. Ten inches down the blade hits something orange and springy and the trencher stalls. The operator climbs down, pulls up a loop of flattened duct, and the street loses its internet for the afternoon. Nobody meant any harm; the cable was simply too shallow to survive ordinary life above it.

Depth of cover is the distance from the finished surface to the top of the duct or cable. Every extra inch is extra digging, extra spoil and extra time, so it is argued over on every job.

## Why depth matters

Depth does four things. It keeps the cable below the reach of casual digging: gardens, fence posts, sprinkler lines and mailbox posts. It spreads vehicle loads so the duct is not crushed under traffic. It keeps the cable below the depth where frost heave and seasonal soil movement are worst. And it leaves room for other utilities to cross above or below with a safe separation.

## Typical requirements

Requirements vary by owner and by permit, so the local rule always governs. That said, the values below are what I most commonly see written into United States specifications, and they are a reasonable starting point for a design.

| Location | Commonly seen minimum cover |
|---|---|
| Customer drop in a lawn | 12 inches |
| Distribution in a sidewalk or grass strip | 24 inches |
| Under a road, measured from the pavement surface | 36 inches |
| Crossing a state highway or railway | 48 inches or more, set by the permit |

Road authorities care about depth more than anyone, because they expect to mill, resurface and widen. A crossing that was fine at 24 inches becomes a hazard when the road is rebuilt with a deeper base.

## India and Australia

In India, the right of way (RoW) permissions for routes along highways commonly specify a much deeper trench, around 1.65 metres, because road widening and drainage works are frequent and the cable must survive them. Inside cities the depth is shallower and often limited by what the road owner will allow and what other services already occupy the footpath. In Australia the convention is pit and pipe, with conduit typically shallower in the footpath and deeper under the carriageway, and the owner's standard sets the figure.

## Proving the depth

Depth is measured from finished grade to the top of the duct, not to the bottom of the trench. The crew should record it at intervals and at every crossing, because the as-built is the only evidence that exists once the trench is closed. Warning tape goes in the backfill above the duct, usually about a foot above it, so that the next excavator sees it before reaching the cable.

Sometimes the specified depth cannot be reached: rock, an existing utility, or a tree root zone. The answer is not to go shallow quietly. It is to agree a protection method with the owner, such as concrete encasement or a steel plate over the duct, and document it. Micro-trenching is shallow by design, which is why it needs its own approval.

## Final thoughts

Depth of cover is cheap insurance paid once, in dirt, against years of accidental damage. Design to the local requirement, measure what was built, and never let a shallow section go unrecorded. What is the shallowest buried fiber you have ever found, and how did it get that way?
