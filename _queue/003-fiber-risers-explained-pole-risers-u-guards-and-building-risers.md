Look at the base of a wooden utility pole at a street corner and you may see a grey or galvanised channel running up from the ground to a little above head height. A cable enters it at the bottom and leaves at the top to join the cables strung along the street. That channel is a riser, and the pole is a riser pole.

Risers are where a network changes from underground to aerial, or climbs between floors of a building. They are easy to overlook on a drawing, yet they are where cables get hit by mowers, vehicles and curious hands.

## Why cables climb a pole

A feeder may run underground along a main road and feed an aerial network in the side streets, or a road crossing may be bored while everything either side is on poles. At each transition the cable leaves the duct through a long-radius sweep at the pole base and rises to the communications attachment height.

The lowest few feet of that climb are exposed. In the United States the National Electrical Safety Code (NESC) requires mechanical protection for cable on a pole where it could be damaged, typically to about eight feet above ground. The two common ways to provide it are U-guards and riser conduit.

## U-guards

A U-guard is a U-shaped channel, usually galvanised steel or rigid plastic, strapped or lagged to the pole over the cable, usually in eight or ten foot lengths. The cable sits inside, covered on three sides. U-guards are quick to fit and easy to retrofit. The weakness is that they are not sealed, so water gets in, and a determined person can lever one open.

## Riser conduit

Riser conduit is a full pipe, usually Schedule 40 or Schedule 80 polyvinyl chloride (PVC) or galvanised rigid steel, mounted on standoff brackets. A sweep at the bottom joins the underground duct and a cap or weatherhead at the top keeps rain out. It gives complete enclosure and better protection against vehicle strikes, but adding a cable later means pulling through the pipe, so I size riser conduit for future cables from the start.

| Feature | U-guard | Riser conduit |
|---|---|---|
| Protection | Three sides, open to pole | Fully enclosed |
| Adding cable later | Easy, lift and lay in | Must pull through |
| Vehicle impact | Fair | Good, especially steel |

On joint-use poles the riser stays in the communications space, below the power attachments, with the clearances the NESC requires. In India, poles are often concrete and the climb is commonly made in galvanised iron (GI) pipe. In Australia the same separation applies, with the detail set by the pole owner.

## Building risers

Inside a multi-storey building, a riser is the vertical pathway between floors: a shaft, a sleeve through each slab, or conduit. In the United States the National Electrical Code (NEC) requires cable in that pathway to be riser-rated, marked OFNR (optical fiber nonconductive riser), or plenum-rated OFNP where the path passes through air-handling space. Every floor penetration must be fire-stopped once the cable is in. In India and Australia the usual expectation is a low smoke zero halogen (LSZH) jacket inside buildings, with the same fire-stopping.

## Final thoughts

A riser is a few feet of hardware that protects thousands of feet of cable on either side of it. Specify the guard or conduit, the sweep radius and the height, and draw it. Which have you had more trouble with in the field, U-guards or riser conduit?
