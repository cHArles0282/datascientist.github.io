A surveyor stands at the base of a pole with a measuring rod extended high over her head, reading off the height of each cable while a colleague types them into a tablet. To an aerial designer those numbers are the whole job, because every one of them is being checked against a clearance rule.

Clearances are the minimum distances that must exist between things on and around a pole: between power and communication cables, between one communication cable and the next, and between the lowest cable and the ground. Get them wrong and the result is either a rejected permit or a hazard.

## Three zones on a pole

Reading a joint-use pole from the top down, there are three zones. The supply space at the top holds the power conductors, transformers and the neutral. The communication space lower down holds telephone, cable television and fiber cables. Between them sits the communication worker safety zone, a band kept deliberately empty so that a technician working on cables below stays well away from anything energised above.

In the United States the National Electrical Safety Code (NESC) sets the safety zone at 40 inches at the pole, measured from the lowest supply conductor, usually the neutral, to the highest communication attachment. At mid-span, where cables sag, the required separation reduces to 30 inches. Streetlight brackets and their wiring count as supply equipment, and they are a common cause of failed clearances.

## Separation between communication cables

Within the communication space the NESC calls for 12 inches between the attachments of different communication cables at the pole. That space gives each company room for its clamps and lets a technician work one cable without leaning on another. Overlashed cables share one strand and therefore one attachment, which is why overlashing is so popular when a pole is crowded.

## Ground and road clearances

The lowest cable must clear whatever is beneath it, measured at the bottom of the sag, not at the pole. The NESC minimums most designers quote are:

| Location below the cable | Minimum clearance for communication cable |
|---|---|
| Roads, streets, alleys and driveways | 15.5 ft |
| Areas accessible to pedestrians only | 9.5 ft |

Those figures apply under the conditions that produce the greatest sag, which means a hot summer day or a heavy ice load depending on the region. Many pole owners and road authorities ask for more than the code minimum, so I always check the local requirement before relying on the code.

## Practice outside the United States

In India the Central Electricity Authority regulations set the ground clearances for overhead lines, and communication cables are expected to sit below the power conductors with a safe separation, but enforcement varies. In Australia overhead line design follows a joint Australian and New Zealand standard, and the electricity distributors specify exactly where communication cables may sit on their poles.

## Final thoughts

Clearances are not a box to tick at the end. They decide the attachment height, which decides whether make-ready is needed, which decides the cost and schedule of the whole build. Measure the pole properly, record the lowest sag, and respect the safety zone as if your own crew will be the next one up that pole.
