Stand at the end of a cul-de-sac with twelve houses on it and you are looking at twelve living units, a few hundred feet of route, one job, and perhaps a day of design time. Depending on the contract, any one of those four things could be the number on the invoice.

Outside plant (OSP) design is bought and sold in several ways, and the billing model shapes the work more than most engineers realise.

## The four common models

| Model | What is counted | Who carries the risk | Where it fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Per job | Each design package, often tiered by size | Vendor, if jobs vary a lot | Drop designs, small reroutes, steady job types |
| Per living unit (LU) | Each home or business passed or served | Shared; vendor on sparse areas, client on dense | Fiber-to-the-home distribution design |
| Per footage | Each foot or metre of designed route | Vendor on dense urban, client on long rural runs | Feeder and backbone routes |
| Hourly | Time spent, at an agreed rate | Client | Studies, rework, undefined scope |

A living unit is a single home, apartment or business that could take service. The term is standard in the US. Australia's National Broadband Network (NBN) says premises, and Indian operators more often say home passed.

## Per-job and per-LU in practice

Per-job billing is simple to administer: one job ordered, one package delivered, one line on the invoice. The weakness is that jobs are not equal. A reroute around one pole and a reroute through a shopping centre are both one job. Most per-job contracts deal with this by tiering jobs into small, medium and large.

Per-LU billing suits fiber-to-the-home (FTTH) programs because the operator thinks in premises anyway. The catch is density. An apartment block delivers hundreds of LUs for a few hours of work; a scattered rural area delivers a handful for days of effort. Contracts usually set different rates by area type, or a minimum per job.

## Per-footage and hourly

Per-footage billing follows the cable. It fits feeder routes, long-haul links and backhaul to mobile towers, where there are few premises but a lot of route to engineer. In the US the unit is the foot; in Australia and India it is the metre, a small detail offshore teams serving both have to keep straight.

Hourly billing, sometimes called time and materials, is the fallback for work that cannot be scoped in advance: feasibility studies, as-built recovery, or rework caused by client-side changes. It is fair, but it gives the client the least cost certainty, so most contracts limit it to defined situations.

## What the model does to behaviour

In my experience, every model pushes behaviour somewhere. Per-job rewards speed on small jobs. Per-LU rewards dense areas and can make teams reluctant to take sparse ones. Per-footage can tempt a designer toward a longer route. Hourly removes the pressure to be efficient. None of this is dishonest; it is just human. The protection is a quality standard and a first-pass acceptance target written into the same contract, so that volume is never paid for twice.

## Final thoughts

There is no single right way to bill design work. The best model matches the kind of work being ordered and makes the risk visible to both sides. Which billing model have you found works best for fiber design, and why?
