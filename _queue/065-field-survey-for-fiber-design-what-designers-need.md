The pole looked fine on the aerial imagery. Straight, clear of trees, well spaced from its neighbours. The surveyor's photo told a different story: a transformer, two power crossings, a cable TV strand at the lowest allowable height, and a guy wire through the only free space. The route moved to the other side of the street that afternoon.

Satellite images and street view are useful, but they are not a survey. The field survey is where the design meets reality.

## What a survey is for

A field survey collects the facts a designer cannot see from the office. It confirms that the proposed route is buildable, captures the data needed to size and place hardware, and flags anything that will need a permit, a special crossing or a change of method. The output feeds directly into the low-level design (LLD), the construction-ready drawing set.

## What designers need from the field

I have settled on a short list of things that must come back from every survey. Missing any one usually means a second trip.

1. Pole data. For each pole: a unique tag, the owner, approximate height, condition, and every existing attachment with its height above ground. Also note guy wires, risers, transformers and streetlights.
2. Spans and clearances. Span length to the next pole, mid-span clearance over roads and driveways, and anything the new cable would cross, such as power lines, railways or water.
3. Underground assets. Manhole and handhole locations, lid condition, how many ducts exist and how many are free, and whether chambers are flooded or silted.
4. Obstacles. Driveways, retaining walls, bridges, culverts, mature trees, fences, and the road surface types that decide between trenching and boring.
5. Photos. A wide shot of each pole or chamber, a close-up of the tag, and a shot in each direction along the route. Photos settle arguments later.
6. Location. A Global Positioning System (GPS) point for every asset, plus a marked-up map where GPS is unreliable under trees or tall buildings.
7. Building details. For drops and multi-dwelling units: entry point, existing conduit and the preferred side for the drop.

## Tools and methods

A measuring wheel still does most of the distance work. Attachment heights are taken with a telescoping measuring rod or a laser rangefinder. Tablets with survey apps have replaced paper forms on most projects and tie each photo to a GPS point automatically.

Practice differs by market. In the United States, pole surveys are detailed because joint-use poles shared with power utilities are governed by the National Electrical Safety Code (NESC), and the make-ready work to create space is a major cost. In India, the survey leans toward right-of-way (RoW) conditions, road surfaces and existing ducts, because much of the network goes underground and permissions from road authorities drive the schedule. In Australia, pit and duct audits were the core of the survey where the National Broadband Network (NBN) reused existing underground infrastructure.

## Common gaps I see

Photos without a reference object. Heights with no note on whether they were measured or estimated. A route walked from one side of the road only. Each looks small, and each has delayed a design.

## Final thoughts

A good survey makes the design almost write itself. A poor one turns every pole into a question. If you are a new surveyor, assume the designer has never seen the street and never will, and capture what you would want in their place.
