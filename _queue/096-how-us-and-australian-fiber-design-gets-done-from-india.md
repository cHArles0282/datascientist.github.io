A field technician photographs a utility pole on a street in Texas at three in the afternoon. By the time he is home for dinner, that photo is on a screen in an Indian design office where the working day has just begun. By the time he is back on the street the next morning, the pole is on a drawing with a strand, a cable and a splice closure on it.

That loop is now ordinary. A large share of outside plant (OSP) design for operators in the United States (US) and Australia is produced in India. Having worked on both sides of it, here is how it actually runs.

## Why the work moved offshore

Three reasons, and cost is only one of them. The second is scale. Fiber-to-the-home (FTTH) programs generate thousands of small, repetitive design jobs, and India has a deep pool of engineering graduates who can be trained as drafters and designers quickly. The third is the clock. India is roughly half a day ahead of the US and a few hours behind Australia, so a job submitted at the end of a US day can be designed overnight and reviewed before the client's next morning. Australian clients see the same effect with an afternoon submission.

## What goes in and what comes out

An offshore design job is only as good as its inputs. The field team in the client country supplies survey data, pole photos, geographic information system (GIS) layers, address lists and the client's design standard. The offshore team returns a drawing package, a bill of materials (BOM), a splice plan and any permit exhibits.

Units are a constant source of small errors. US jobs are in feet, from pole heights to cable orders. Australian and Indian jobs are in metres. A designer switching clients in the same week has to be careful, and good teams fix the units in templates so nobody has to think about it.

## What makes it work

1. A written design standard from the client, with examples of accepted work.
2. A trained offshore lead who has seen the client's streets, even if only through photos and street-level imagery.
3. A two-stage quality check, one offshore and one onshore, with defects logged by category.
4. A shared tracker so both sides see the same job status and the same clock.
5. A daily overlap window, usually early morning in the US or late afternoon in Australia, when questions can be answered live.
6. Secure handling of client data, since pole data and customer addresses are sensitive.

## Where it gets hard

The hardest part is context. A designer in India may never have stood under a US pole line or opened an Australian pit. Local practice around pole attachments, clearances and traffic management is learned second-hand. The best offshore teams close that gap with photos, with onshore reviewers who explain why a comment was made, and by sending leads to client sites when possible. The weakest treat the standard as a set of symbols and never ask why.

Response time matters too. A query that sits unanswered for a US business day costs the offshore team a whole shift.

## Final thoughts

Offshore design works when it is treated as one team across two or three time zones, not as a vendor at the end of an email. The programs I have seen go well all had people on both sides who could pick up the phone. What has been your experience with design work delivered across time zones?
