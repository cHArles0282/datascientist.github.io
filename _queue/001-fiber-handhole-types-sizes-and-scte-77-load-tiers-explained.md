Walk along almost any suburban sidewalk and you will step over a rectangular lid set flush with the concrete, often with "COMMUNICATIONS" or "FIBER OPTIC" cast into it. For an outside plant (OSP) designer, that lid belongs to a handhole, and choosing the right one is one of the first decisions on any underground route.

A handhole (HH) is a shallow below-grade box that gives access to cable and duct without anyone climbing inside. Everything about it, from wall material to lid rating, follows from where it sits and what might drive over it.

## What a handhole is for

Handholes do three jobs in a fiber network. They hold splice closures where distribution cables branch. They store slack loops so a technician can lift a closure to the surface for re-entry. And they act as pull points, breaking a long duct run into manageable pulls. In my experience the slack storage is the part new designers underestimate.

## Materials you will see

Polymer concrete is a resin-bound aggregate, usually fiberglass reinforced. It is strong, light enough to hand carry in small sizes, and the most common choice for sidewalk handholes in the United States.

Fiberglass reinforced plastic (FRP) is lighter again and resists corrosion, so it suits remote sites and aggressive soils.

High-density polyethylene (HDPE) is the lightest and cheapest. It is fine for small boxes in lawns and at drop locations, but not anywhere a vehicle might cross.

Precast concrete is heavy and needs a crane or excavator to set, but it is the default under roadways and for large sizes.

In India, reinforced cement concrete (RCC) or brick chambers cast on site are still common, built by the civil contractor rather than bought as a product. In Australia the same enclosure is called a pit, and operators standardise on numbered pit sizes.

## Common sizes

Sizes are quoted as nominal inside length by width in inches, then depth. The ones I see most often are 12 by 12, 17 by 30, 24 by 36 and 30 by 48, with depths from 12 to 36 inches. A 24 by 36 or 30 by 48 box is where I would expect a distribution closure with full slack loops. Bigger is not always better; a large box in a narrow grass strip conflicts with every other utility in that strip.

## Load ratings: SCTE 77 tiers and H-20

ANSI/SCTE 77 is the North American standard for underground enclosure integrity. It defines load tiers named for their design load in thousands of pounds.

| Tier | Design load | Typical placement |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 5 | 5,000 lb | Lawns and pedestrian areas only |
| Tier 8 | 8,000 lb | Sidewalks away from vehicles |
| Tier 15 | 15,000 lb | Driveways, occasional non-deliberate vehicle traffic |
| Tier 22 | 22,500 lb | Driveways and parking, deliberate vehicle traffic |

AASHTO H-20 is different. It is a highway loading from the American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials, and it describes a design truck, not a box. SCTE 77 says its tiers are for off-roadway locations, so when a handhole must sit in a travelled lane I specify a precast concrete unit with an H-20 rated frame and cover.

## Final thoughts

The cheapest handhole is the one that never has to be dug up and replaced. Match the material to the handling, the size to the slack, and the tier to the heaviest vehicle that could ever park on it. What is the most unexpected thing you have seen driving over a sidewalk handhole?
