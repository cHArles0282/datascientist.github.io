Next to a highway embankment or a railway line you will occasionally see a deep, square pit shored with steel plates, and at the bottom of it a machine that looks like a giant drill on rails. Spoil comes out one end. A fat steel pipe disappears into the earth wall at the other. Nothing is happening on the road or track above, which is exactly the point.

This is jack and bore, sometimes called auger boring. It is the method that highway departments and railway companies most often insist on when fiber has to cross their property.

## How the method works

Jack and bore is not steerable in the way directional drilling is. It relies on a straight, accurately set-up launch and a stiff steel casing to hold line and grade. A typical crossing runs like this:

1. Dig a jacking pit on one side of the crossing and a smaller receiving pit on the other, both deep enough to put the casing at the required depth.
2. Set a track and thrust block in the jacking pit, aligned precisely to the design line and grade.
3. Push the first section of steel casing into the face while a rotating auger inside it carries the spoil back into the pit.
4. Weld on the next casing section and repeat until the casing breaks through into the receiving pit.
5. Install the carrier conduits inside the casing on spacers, then seal both ends.

The casing is the permanent structure. The fiber conduits inside it can be added to, pulled out, or replaced later without touching the crossing again.

## Why owners require a casing

A highway or railway owner cares about two things: settlement and future access. Settlement under a road or track, even a small amount, is a safety problem. A steel casing pushed through undisturbed ground, with spoil removed only as fast as the casing advances, creates very little void and therefore very little risk of the surface dropping.

Future access matters because the owner does not want to open the crossing twice. A casing with spare capacity lets later conduits be added from the pits. Railway crossings in the United States are typically reviewed against guidance from the American Railway Engineering and Maintenance-of-Way Association (AREMA), and the railroad's own engineering staff approve the plan. State departments of transportation have similar rules for limited-access highways. In India, crossings under railway lines go through Indian Railways' approval process, and in Australia the relevant rail operator and state road authority each set their own conditions. In every case, budget months for approvals, not weeks.

## Jack and bore against directional drilling

Horizontal directional drilling (HDD) is cheaper and faster for most crossings, so the question is why anyone would dig two pits and push steel. The answer is control. HDD leaves an annulus of drilling fluid around the conduit, it can wander off line in mixed ground, and a frac-out under a railway is a serious incident. Jack and bore gives a straight, cased hole with the surface untouched. Where the owner allows HDD with a steel casing, the choice becomes a cost question; where they do not, the decision has been made for you.
