Two aerial fiber routes can look identical from the sidewalk. Get closer and the difference is obvious. One hangs beneath a steel strand, wrapped every few inches by a spiral of lashing wire. The other hangs on its own, gripped at each pole by a helical dead-end with nothing above it.

The second cable is ADSS. Choosing between ADSS and lashed cable is one of the first design decisions on an aerial fiber project.

## What makes ADSS different

ADSS stands for all-dielectric self-supporting. The cable contains no metal at all. Its strength comes from aramid yarns under the outer jacket, which carry the tension across the span, while the fibers sit in loose tubes around a non-metallic central member. Because nothing in it conducts, the cable needs no bonding or grounding.

That property also lets ADSS be installed in the supply space on power poles, close to energised conductors, as long as the jacket is track-resistant. Tracking is a slow surface erosion caused by the electric field near high-voltage lines, and an ordinary jacket can fail from it over the years.

ADSS hangs on its own hardware. At dead-ends a preformed helical grip wraps around the jacket and transfers the tension into a thimble on the pole. Sag and tension must be calculated for the specific cable, because over-tensioning stresses the fibers and under-tensioning breaks clearances at mid-span.

## What lashed cable gives you

Lashed cable relies on a galvanized steel messenger strand to carry the load, with the fiber cable lashed to it by stainless wire. The cable itself can be a cheaper, lighter design because it carries no tension, and adding a second or third cable later is easy: the crew overlashes the new cable onto the existing bundle.

The cost is more parts and more rules. Strand must be tensioned, guyed, bonded and grounded, and the whole assembly must stay in the communication space.

## Side by side

| Attribute | ADSS | Lashed to strand |
|---|---|---|
| Strength member | Aramid yarn inside the cable | Steel messenger strand |
| Metal in the system | None | Yes, must be bonded and grounded |
| Adding cables later | Each new cable needs its own hardware | Overlash onto existing bundle |
| Supply-space installation | Possible with track-resistant jacket | Not permitted |
| Span lengths | Long spans possible | Limited by strand and sag |
| Installation effort | Pull, tension, few parts | Strand first, then lash |

## Which one, and where

In the United States, lashed cable remains the default for telephone and cable operators because the strand is often already there. ADSS appears on electric utility routes, long river or highway crossings, and rural broadband builds with no existing strand.

In India, self-supporting cable dominates. ADSS is common on longer spans, and figure-8 cable, which has a steel messenger moulded into the same jacket as the fiber, is everywhere on last-mile routes. In Australia the pole owner's rules usually decide.

## Final thoughts

Neither design is better in every case. Lashed cable rewards routes that will keep growing, while ADSS rewards routes with no existing strand, long spans or a need to sit in the power space.
