A trench for a fiber handhole on a road shoulder looks simple from a passing car. Get closer and the details tell you whether the crew knows what it is doing. A ladder sticks out of the excavation. The spoil pile sits well back from the edge. If the cut is deep, a steel box or sloped sides hold the ground back. These are not habits. They are federal rules.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) is the United States Department of Labor agency that writes and enforces workplace safety standards. A handful of its rules shape nearly every day a telecom crew spends in the field, and a project manager who understands them plans better jobs.

## Two rulebooks for one crew

OSHA has a dedicated telecommunications standard within its general industry rules, found at 29 CFR 1910.268. It covers pole climbing, aerial lifts, manhole and vault entry, cable handling and tools. Construction work, including trenching and new-build excavation, falls under the construction standards at 29 CFR 1926. A fiber crew can move between the two in a single day, placing conduit under one rulebook in the morning and splicing from a bucket under the other after lunch.

My practical rule is that whichever standard is stricter for the task at hand is the one to meet.

## Trenches and excavations

Excavation is where telecom work kills people, and the construction standard is specific about it. Widely published requirements include:

1. A competent person, trained to identify hazards and with authority to stop work, inspects the excavation daily and after rain or any change in conditions.
2. Trenches 5 feet deep or more need a protective system such as sloping, shoring or a trench box, unless the excavation is entirely in stable rock.
3. Trenches 4 feet deep or more need a ladder, ramp or other means of exit within 25 feet of lateral travel.
4. Spoil and equipment stay at least 2 feet back from the edge so their weight does not load the trench wall.
5. Utilities are located before digging and protected or supported once exposed.
6. Atmospheric testing is required where oxygen deficiency or hazardous gas could reasonably be expected, including many vaults and manholes.

Most fiber trenches are shallow, which tempts crews to skip these steps. A collapse in a chest-deep trench can still crush a person.

## Poles and aerial lifts

The telecom standard requires a pole to be tested for soundness before anyone climbs it, since rot at the ground line is invisible from the road. Climbers must use approved climbing equipment and fall protection, and anyone working aloft must be trained. In an aerial lift the worker wears a body harness with a lanyard attached to the bucket or boom, never to the pole or strand, and the truck is set on outriggers before the boom goes up.

Personal protective equipment is the baseline on every site: hard hat, high-visibility clothing, eye protection and gloves. Roadside work adds traffic control, which deserves a post of its own.

Australia regulates the same hazards under state work health and safety laws built on a national model. India has construction safety rules on paper, but in my experience enforcement on telecom sites depends heavily on the operator's own standards.

## Final thoughts

OSHA rules are often described as paperwork. On a fiber project they are really a set of design decisions: how deep the trench is, which pole gets climbed and what the crew carries on the truck.
