Stand at a residential corner during a fiber build and count what is already under the ground. A gas main runs down one side. A water main runs down the other. Service lines cross the strip every few houses. Power may be buried too, and sewer laterals from every home slope to the main in the middle of the road.

Fiber is the last utility to arrive and has to fit between all of them. On projects I've worked on, most of the design effort in an underground build goes not into the fiber itself but into avoiding the things already there.

## Each utility fails differently

A conflict is any place where the fiber path crosses or runs close to another utility, and each utility fails in its own way.

Gas is the one that worries me most. A nicked plastic gas service may not leak right away, and a leak can migrate along a conduit into a basement. Locate paint for gas is yellow, and crews treat it as the highest-risk crossing.

Power is marked red. Striking a buried primary cable can kill the operator, and even a secondary service can arc through a steel drill rod. Water mains, marked blue, are less of a life risk, but a broken main floods the trench and can undermine the road. Sewers, marked green, are the quiet problem because they are rarely located accurately, if at all.

## The cross bore problem

Horizontal directional drilling (HDD) is the standard method for installing fiber conduit under lawns and streets without open trenching. The drill head steers under the surface and the operator cannot see what it passes through. If the bore goes through a sewer lateral, the conduit may sit inside the pipe for years without anyone knowing. This is called a cross bore.

The danger comes later. A homeowner calls a plumber about a slow drain, and the plumber runs a cutting tool down the pipe. If the cross-bored line is gas, the result can be an explosion. Fiber conduit carries no gas, but a fiber cross bore still blocks the sewer and must be dug out at the operator's expense. Many utilities now require camera inspection of laterals where bores cross them.

## Designing the conflict out

The sequence I follow on an underground design is roughly this:

1. Request utility records and overlay them on the base map before choosing a route.
2. Walk the route and note every meter, valve box, manhole and pedestal, since surface features reveal buried lines.
3. Pick the side of the street with the fewest known utilities, even if it costs more footage.
4. Show every crossing on the drawing with a required pothole, meaning the existing line is exposed by hand or vacuum excavation before the bore passes.
5. Specify the bore depth and the minimum separation required by the utility owner and the local permit, which vary by jurisdiction.
6. Note that the crew stops and calls engineering if the field does not match the drawing.

In the United States the utility owner and the city permit set separation rules, and Australian practice is similar. In India, where records are thinner and trenching by hand is common, trial pits along the route do much of the work that records do elsewhere.

## Final thoughts

A good underground fiber design is mostly a map of what to avoid. The route that looks longest on paper is often the safest and cheapest one to build.
