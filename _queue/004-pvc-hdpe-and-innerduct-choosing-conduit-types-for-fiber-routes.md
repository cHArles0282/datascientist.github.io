Stand beside an open trench on a city street and you will see grey rigid pipes stacked neatly on plastic spacers. Cross the road and a reel of black pipe with an orange stripe is feeding into a directional drill. Both are conduit. Both will carry fiber. They are different products, chosen for different reasons, and a designer needs to know which to call for where.

Conduit is the pathway that protects a cable and lets it be replaced or added to without digging again. In my experience the pathway decision outlives the cable decision by decades.

## Rigid PVC

Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) conduit comes in straight sticks, typically ten or twenty feet long, joined with solvent cement. Schedule 40 and Schedule 80 describe wall thickness, with 80 the heavier wall for exposed locations and risers. For telecom duct banks there are also thinner-walled EB (encased burial) and DB (direct burial) grades. Bends are factory sweeps with a set radius.

PVC is the material of open-cut trench and concrete-encased duct banks in dense urban areas, where multiple four-inch ducts are stacked between manholes. It is stiff, so it needs a straight trench and does not suit directional drilling. Colour conventions differ by country. In Australia, white PVC is the convention for communications and orange for electrical, which helps anyone opening the ground later.

## HDPE

High-density polyethylene (HDPE) duct is flexible and arrives on reels in long continuous lengths. Wall thickness is described by the standard dimension ratio (SDR), the outside diameter divided by the wall thickness, so a lower number means a thicker wall. SDR 11 and SDR 13.5 are common. The inner wall may be smooth, ribbed, or pre-lubricated to cut friction. Lengths are joined with mechanical couplers or by butt fusion.

Because it bends and comes in long lengths, HDPE is what goes in behind a directional drill, a cable plough or a micro-trencher. It is also what most microduct is made from. In India, permanently lubricated (PLB) HDPE duct in 40/33 mm and 50/42 mm sizes (outside and inside diameter) is the standard product for both long-haul and access routes, and most cable is blown into it rather than pulled.

## Innerduct

Innerduct is a smaller duct placed inside a larger conduit to divide it into separate pathways. A typical arrangement is three 1.25 inch innerducts inside a four-inch PVC duct. Each innerduct carries one cable, so a new cable can be installed later without rubbing against those already there. Innerduct is also pulled into half-empty duct banks to recover capacity.

There are three kinds. Corrugated HDPE innerduct is light and bends easily. Smooth-wall HDPE pulls with less friction. Fabric innerduct is a flat textile sleeve with several cells; it packs more pathways into the same conduit because it has no round wall taking up space.

| Type | Form | Joined by | Best suited to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rigid PVC | Straight sticks | Solvent cement | Open trench, encased duct banks, risers |
| HDPE | Continuous reels | Couplers or fusion | Directional drilling, ploughing, long rural runs |
| Innerduct | Reels, inside host duct | Couplers | Subdividing large conduit, reusing old duct |

Galvanised rigid steel also appears on bridge attachments and where vehicle impact is likely.

## Final thoughts

Choose PVC where you can trench straight and may encase, HDPE where you drill or plough, and innerduct wherever a big pipe would otherwise hold one lonely cable. Which duct type has caused you the most trouble on an installation, and why?
