The first time you strip a fiber cable it feels like peeling an onion with a knife you are afraid to use. Score the black jacket, pull the ripcord, and out come layers of yarn, tape, a stiff rod down the middle and a bundle of colored tubes that look far too fragile to carry a city's internet. Each layer is there for a reason.

On projects I have worked on, most cable damage traces back to someone not respecting one of these layers, usually the strength member or the bend radius.

## From the outside in

This is the order you meet them in a typical loose tube outside plant cable.

1. Outer jacket. Usually black polyethylene, which resists sunlight and moisture. Printed length marks let crews record footage at each closure.
2. Ripcord. A thin, strong thread under the jacket. Pull it and the jacket splits cleanly without a blade touching the tubes.
3. Armor, if present. A corrugated steel tape for crush and rodent protection. It must be bonded and grounded at closures.
4. Water-blocking layer. Either swellable tape and yarn (a dry cable) or gel (a flooded cable). If water enters a cut, the blocking swells and stops it travelling along the core.
5. Strength yarn. Aramid fibers that take the pulling tension during installation so the glass never does.
6. Buffer tubes. Small plastic tubes, colored in the standard twelve-color sequence, each holding up to twelve coated fibers. Unused positions are filled with solid filler rods so the core stays round.
7. Central strength member. A glass-reinforced plastic rod or steel wire at the center that resists bending and cold-weather contraction. All-dielectric designs are preferred near power lines.

## The fibers themselves

Inside each tube the fibers are tiny. The glass core and cladding together measure 125 microns, roughly the width of a coarse human hair, and a soft acrylate coating brings each fiber to about 250 microns. The coating is colored to identify the fiber. Gel or dry powder inside the tube keeps water away from the glass, and that is why every splice begins with a cleaning wipe.

## Fiber counts and what they mean

Cables are built in multiples of twelve. Common counts in the field are 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 144 and 288. Feeder and backbone routes go higher, and ribbon construction pushes well past a thousand fibers in a single sheath.

A distribution cable serving a neighborhood might need only 24 or 48 fibers, while the feeder behind it may need 288 or more. In the US it is common to install more fibers than the immediate need to avoid a second dig. In India and Australia the same logic applies, though duct space and budget often push designs toward tighter counts.

## Handling rules that follow from the construction

The construction explains the field rules. Pull on the strength member, never on the tubes. Keep the cable above its minimum bend radius, usually quoted as a multiple of cable diameter. Seal cut ends on gel-filled cables, since an open end invites water. Ground the armor if there is any.

## Final thoughts

A fiber cable is mostly protection. The glass is a small fraction of the cross-section, and everything around it exists to keep tension, water, rodents and bends away. Once you can name the layers, the data sheet stops looking like a parts list and starts reading like a set of promises.
