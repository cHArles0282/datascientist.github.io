Walk down almost any street that is getting fiber and you can read the stage of the project from the pavement. Fresh spray paint in orange and white means a locate request has gone in. Pink flags on wire stakes mean someone has surveyed the route. A row of hand holes with fresh backfill means construction is well along.

In my experience, the stages behind those marks are the same everywhere, even when the paperwork and the vocabulary change from country to country. Here is how a fiber project moves from an idea to a finished record.

## Survey and design

Everything starts with a field survey. A surveyor walks or drives the route and records what is actually there: pole numbers and heights, existing cables, duct banks, hand holes, driveways, trees and anything else that will shape the build. In the United States (US) this is often called fielding. In India and Australia the term site survey is more common.

The survey feeds outside plant (OSP) design. The designer chooses the route, places splice closures and fiber distribution terminals, sizes the cable, and works out the splitter layout for a passive optical network (PON). The output is a drawing package, a bill of materials (BOM) and a splice plan.

## Permitting and approvals

A design is not buildable until someone with authority says it can go in the ground or on the pole. In the US that usually means a municipal right-of-way permit, a pole attachment application to the pole owner, and sometimes a railroad or state highway crossing permit. Pole attachments trigger make-ready work, where existing attachments are moved to create clearance.

In India the equivalent is Right of Way (RoW) permission from the municipal body or highway authority, and it is often the longest step in the whole project. In Australia, much of the network runs through existing pits and ducts, so access agreements with the duct owner matter as much as council permits.

## Construction

Construction crews place conduit, pull or lash cable, and splice fibers. Aerial builds strand and lash cable along existing poles. Underground builds trench, directional bore, or use micro-trenching along the road edge. Once cable is in place, splicers open closures, splice according to the plan, and testers confirm loss with an optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) and a power meter.

| Stage | Main output | Typical cause of delay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survey | Field notes, photos, pole data | Access to private property |
| Design | Drawings, BOM, splice plan | Incomplete survey data |
| Permitting | Approved permits, make-ready orders | Agency review queues |
| Construction | Placed cable, splices, test results | Weather, utility conflicts |
| As-built | Redline and final records | Low priority once service is live |

## As-built and closeout

The as-built is the record of what was actually built, not what was designed. Crews mark changes on a redline copy, and the design team updates the drawings and the geographic information system (GIS) database. This is the step most often rushed, and it is the one the next project depends on. A wrong as-built becomes a wrong survey for the next build.

## Final thoughts

Each stage hands something to the next, and most problems trace back to a hand-off that was incomplete. The teams that deliver consistently are the ones that treat survey quality and as-built quality as seriously as the design itself.
