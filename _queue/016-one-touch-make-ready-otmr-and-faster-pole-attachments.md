Picture a pole that needs three cables moved before a new fiber line can be attached. Under the traditional approach, the cable company sends a truck one week, the telephone company sends one the next month, and a third attacher gets to it when it can. The same pole is visited three times, traffic is controlled three times, and the new attacher waits for the slowest party.

One-touch make-ready, usually shortened to OTMR, was created to end that cycle. One crew touches the pole once and does all of the simple rearrangement at the same time.

## The problem it solves

Make-ready is the work of shifting existing attachments so a new cable fits. Under traditional rules every attacher moves its own cable, in sequence, on its own schedule. The result, in my experience, is that make-ready becomes the longest and least predictable stage of an aerial fiber build.

## How OTMR works, and what it leaves out

Under OTMR the new attacher hires a contractor that the pole owner has already approved. That contractor surveys the poles, prepares the make-ready design, and then performs all of the simple make-ready work in the communication space in a single visit, including moving cables that belong to other companies.

The process is built around notice. Existing attachers are told when the survey will happen and are invited to attend. After the design is complete, they receive the plan and a window to object or to do the work themselves. Once the work is done they are notified again and can inspect the result. The new attacher is liable for any damage.

OTMR is limited to simple make-ready in the communication space. It does not include work in the supply space near power conductors, which stays with the electric utility, and it does not include complex make-ready such as pole replacements, relocations that need splicing, or moves likely to cause an outage.

## Where the rules come from

Cities such as Louisville and Nashville adopted local OTMR ordinances in 2016. In 2018 the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) adopted OTMR nationally for simple make-ready on poles under its jurisdiction. States that regulate their own pole attachments, and poles owned by cooperatives and municipal utilities, are not automatically covered, so the rules still vary from place to place.

There is no direct equivalent in India or Australia, where make-ready is negotiated with the pole owner. The idea of a single contractor doing all the rearrangement under one permission is still useful.

## Traditional and OTMR compared

| Aspect | Traditional make-ready | One-touch make-ready |
|---|---|---|
| Who moves existing cables | Each attacher, separately | One approved contractor |
| Number of pole visits | One per attacher | One in total |
| Scope | Simple and complex work | Simple work in communication space only |
| Schedule control | Depends on slowest attacher | Largely with the new attacher |
| Risk for existing attachers | Handle their own cable | Rely on notice, inspection and liability rules |

## Final thoughts

OTMR shifts the balance between speed and control. New attachers gain months. Existing attachers give up some say over who handles their plant. Whether that trade is fair depends on how well the notice and inspection steps are followed.

If you were an existing attacher, would you be comfortable with another company's contractor moving your cable?
